# 29. Elevation, the paper metaphor, and containment

*Material Design's three successive elevation models, how six other systems express containment, and what the perception and instructional-design literature measures about grouping by enclosure. Companion to memo 28 (colour and wayfinding); the colour-role, contrast and dark-mode material there is not repeated. No design proposed. Compiled 22 September 2026.*

**The problem.** A dense B2B app whose pages are walls of rows with no containers: tables float on the page, sections have no boundary, and a previous attempt set surface tone values without a model of what a container *is*.

**Tiers.** 1 = peer-reviewed or published method and sample. 2 = first-party documentation. 3 = practitioner.

**Fetch notes.** Fetched 22 Sep 2026. **Material 3's own specification was reached for the first time in these memos.** `m3.material.io` still serves only a 61 KB Angular shell, in Wayback too — but its bundle (`main.3cea039dae70f026.js`) holds the route table and content endpoint: pages are served as JSON from `/_dsm/content/m3/2026-09-16_06-10-03/{fileId}.json`. Every M3 quote comes from there. Material 1 and 2 come from Wayback snapshots of `material.io`, server-rendered at 2018–2019 timestamps but not 2020. Sage is still 403; Tullis is quoted from Crossref, Palmer and Cowan from NCBI E-utilities. Polaris from Wayback. Atlassian publishes a machine-readable spec at `atlassian.design/DESIGN.md` stating rules its human pages only imply.

## Part A. Material's three elevation models

### A1. Material 1 (2014): elevation *is* paper, shadow is the only cue

"A material metaphor is the unifying theory of a rationalized space and a system of motion. The material is grounded in tactile reality, **inspired by the study of paper and ink**." The sheet has immutable physics — "Solid / Occupies unique points in space / Impenetrable… Can join to other material / Can separate, split, and heal" — and fixed thickness, "a uniform thickness (1dp)." Four rules bear on containment: "**Material is solid. Input events cannot pass through material**"; "Multiple material elements cannot occupy the same point in space simultaneously"; "**Material cannot pass through other material**… when changing elevation"; and the prohibition the later argument overturns, "**Shadows are never approximated by coloring material**." The monopoly is explicit: "Shadows provide important visual cues about objects' depth and directional movement. **They are the only visual cue indicating the amount of separation between surfaces.**" M1 coins the surviving vocabulary — resting elevation, "the default elevation that does not change," and dynamic elevation offsets, "the goal elevation that a component moves towards, relative to the component's resting state" — makes desktop shallower by rule ("Desktop resting elevation is 2dp below the listed values"), and treats nesting as inheritance: "Children inherit transformative properties from their parent, such as position, rotation, scale, and elevation," and "other objects do not get inserted between parents and children."

### A2. Material 2 (2018): the same table, shadow loses its monopoly

"Elevation can be depicted using shadows or other visual cues, such as surface fills or opacities." A surface must show "Surface edges… Overlap with other surfaces… Distance from other surfaces," and the devices are ranked: "**Shadows show surface edges, surface overlap, and the degree of elevation. Different surface colors show surface edges and overlap, but not the degree of elevation. Opacity shows surface edges and overlap, but not the degree of elevation.**" M2 names the brief's failure mode: "The lack of shadows or other visual cues to indicate edges and surface overlap creates insufficient contrast between surfaces. As a result, **it's difficult to understand which pieces of this UI are grouped together**." It sets a contrast floor — "Edges must create sufficient contrast between surfaces (by meeting or exceeding accessible contrast ratios)" — bans decoration ("Don't use shadows for style only"), and concedes the desktop case: "**cards at 0dp elevation on desktop are outlined with a stroke**."

Resting elevations and dynamic offsets (dp): **24** Dialog; **16** Modal bottom sheet, Modal side sheet, Navigation drawer; **12** FAB (pressed); **8** Standard bottom sheet, Standard side sheet, Bottom navigation bar, Bottom app bar, Menus and sub menus, Card (picked up), Contained button (pressed); **6** FAB (resting), Snackbar; **4** Top app bar (scrolled); **0 or 4** Top app bar (resting); **3** Refresh indicator, Search bar (scrolled); **2** Contained button (resting); **1** Search bar (resting), Card (resting), Switch; **0** Text button, Standard side sheet.

*Cards*: "Cards are contained, independent, and inseparable… **A card cannot merge with another card, or divide into multiple cards.**" *Dividers* gives the cheapest-device rule: "**Dividers should only be used if elements cannot be separated using white space.**" "Use dividers sparingly, to create groupings rather than separate items." "Don't use dividers to separate individual items."

M2's dark theme is where tone first replaces shadow: "**The higher a surface's elevation… the lighter that surface becomes**," via "a semi-transparent overlay," because "an overlay… makes it easier to distinguish elevation between components and to see shadows." Its ramp is the only numeric tonal ladder any system publishes — 0dp 0%, 1dp 5%, 2dp 7%, 3dp 8%, 4dp 9%, 6dp 11%, 8dp 12%, 12dp 14%, 16dp 15%, 24dp 16% — with two carve-outs: overlays are "not applied to component surfaces that use primary or secondary colors," and "shadows remain dark… Don't use light glows in place of dark shadows."

### A3. Material 3 (current): containment split from elevation, surface tint deprecated

*Elevation* (updated 2026-07-17) opens: "**Tokens have no shadows or color; each platform determines the specific shadows and values to use at each elevation level** / Elevation can be shown as tonal surface colors or shadows / Avoid changing the default elevation of Material 3 components / Stick to using a small amount of elevation levels." What changed from M2: "**Shadows**: Instead of applying shadows by default to all levels, use shadows only when required to create additional protection against a background or to encourage interaction. **Color**: New color mappings… **Levels**: Elevation is now described in terms of levels."

"Material uses six levels of elevation… 0, +1, +2, +3, +4, and +5. An element's resting state can be on levels 0 to +3, while levels +4 and +5 are reserved for user-interacted states such as hover and dragged." (The same diagram's alt text says "the 5 elevation levels"; the page contradicts itself about whether zero counts.) dp: 0=0, 1=1, 2=3, 3=6, 4=8, 5=12. "Material 3's elevation system is deliberately limited to just a handful of levels."

Tone is now default, shadow the exception: "**By default, Material 3's surfaces use tonal difference to indicate separation.** Other methods can be used to indicate edges, such as: Giving surfaces a drop shadow / Placing a scrim behind a surface." "When it comes to applying shadows, less is more. The fewer levels in your UI, the more power they have to direct attention and action." Shadow gets exactly two jobs: "**Protect elements** — When a background is patterned or visually busy, the hairline style might not provide sufficient protection," and "**Encourage interaction** — Elements can temporarily lift on focus, selection, or another kind of interaction." "Scrims use the scrim color role at an opacity of 32%."

Then the divorce. *Elevation*: "You can pick from a range of surface and surface container color roles. **These roles are not tied to elevation, and provide flexibility for defining containment areas.** Any overlapping containment areas or components should have different color roles in order to visually communicate separation." *Color system*: "**Tone-based surface color roles have replaced the previous approach of surfaces at +1 to +5 elevation.**" And in the tokens section: "**Surface tint color is deprecated. Use elevation level tokens (0–5) instead.**" Component elevation "is only used to determine where the component sits in relation to other components… Elevation has no shadow or value of its own by default." So: **containment is a colour-role decision, elevation is a z-order decision, and the tonal-overlay mechanism that was M3's headline feature in 2021 is deprecated** — correcting what memo 28 recorded second-hand as "Material 3 uses tint."

The roles (*Color roles*, 2026-07-27): "Surface: Default color for backgrounds / On surface / On surface variant," plus "five surface container roles named based on their level of emphasis: Surface container lowest… low… Surface container: Default container color… high… highest." The nesting sentence: "Surface container is the default role, but the others are especially helpful for **creating hierarchy and nested containers in layouts for expanded screens**." Baseline pairing is "surface for a background area and surface container for a navigation area," held stable — "All color mappings – but especially surface colors – should remain the same for layout regions across breakpoints." The add-ons `surface-dim` and `surface-bright` "keep their relative brightness across both light and dark theme," and are rationed: "If you aren't sure whether your product should use the add-on roles, it probably shouldn't." Boundaries get two tokens and four prohibitions, of which two matter here: "**Don't use the outline color for components that contain multiple elements, such as cards. Instead, use outline variant**," and "Don't use the outline variant color to create visual hierarchy or define the visual boundary of targets." The layout page names both strategies: "panes can blend in with the background. This is called **implicit grouping**… **Explicit grouping** uses distinct colors or outlines to visually delineate content."

The app bar is the clearest before/after: "M2: Elevation and a drop shadow raise the top app bar when content is present underneath. **M3: On scroll, a color fill overlay separates the app bar from the content beneath.**"

## Part B. Containment in six other systems

**Fluent 2** has no layering model — only a generated shadow ramp ("shadow 2 has 2 pixel blur and shadow 64 has 64 pixel blur"), low (2/4/8/16) and high (28/64): shadow 4 for "Cards, grid items, and list items," 8 for "Floating action buttons, raised cards, and raised app bars," 16 for "Callouts and hover cards," 28 for "Bottom sheet, side navigation," 64 for "Pop-up dialogs" and "Panels." Separately, four *materials* — solid, acrylic, mica, smoke — where "Solid is the most common… an opaque material that uses color and varying elevation to highlight different UI regions." Its grouping doctrine is whitespace-first: "**Space is used to denote groups of associated information. Used correctly, spacing creates logical sections of content on a page without having to use lines or other graphical elements as a divider.**" It flags the dense-wall risk both ways: "UI elements that are set close to each other might be overlooked. People may notice the grouping but not process each individual item," and "Too much dense information can also be disorienting and overwhelming."

**IBM Carbon** is the only system with a code-enforced layering model. "There are four layers within a theme: base layer, layer 01, layer 02, and layer 03… Each step in UI color (excluding interaction colors) is another layer." What counts is mechanical: "**A field is considered a layer on top of the background it is placed on.**" Its worked example is a dense page: background base; tabs layer 01; "the text input field is placed on top of `$layer-01` making it a part of the next layer level"; tiles layer 02; "components added on top of the tiles—the text input and overflow menu—are considered part of third layer level." The `<Layer>` component automates it ("A contextual token is aware of what layer it is placed on") with a hard ceiling: "**Components can be nested inside the layer component up to three level.**" Carbon's card-equivalent is unelevated: "Use tiles to group related information in flexible containers… **Tiles reside on the same plane as the page background layer and do not have elevation.** … **Do not add a drop shadow to tiles.**"

**Atlassian** has four planes plus an overflow shadow: "Sunken / Default / Raised / Overlay." Default is flat and bordered: "**To create flat cards, pair with a border.**" Raised is rationed: "reserved for cards that can be moved… **Raised elevations can create visual noise, so don't use to group content when a border or white space would suffice.**" Sunken is the grouping tool and carries the nesting rule: "The sunken surface creates a backdrop (or well) where other content sits. Columns on a Kanban board are a good example… **Only use sunken surfaces on the default surface level. Don't apply sunken elevations on raised or overlay elevations. To differentiate areas of the UI in other ways, use whitespace or borders instead.**" Pairing is mandatory ("Always pair `elevation.surface.raised` with `elevation.shadow.raised`"). Its machine-readable spec adds the canvas rule — "**MUST NOT** use `surface-sunken` as the page background to create contrast against cards… '**Grey page, white cards' is the common drift pattern; the Atlassian pattern is 'white page, bordered cards, optional sunken wells inside**'" — a quota ("**At most one raised region per screen**"), an ordering rule ("**SHOULD** prefer whitespace and borders for grouping before reaching for elevation"), and a named anti-pattern ("**MUST NOT** imitate Material's depth theatre (floating cards everywhere)").

**Apple** treats materials as hierarchy-through-translucency — "By allowing color to pass through from background to foreground, a material establishes visual hierarchy to help people more easily retain a sense of place" — and splits the stack: "Liquid Glass forms a distinct functional layer for controls and navigation elements… **Don't use Liquid Glass in the content layer**," which uses standard materials. Its container is the Box, and its guidance is the most explicit anti-nesting statement anywhere: "A box creates a visually distinct group of logically related information and components. By default, a box uses a visible border or background color to separate its contents." "**Prefer keeping a box relatively small in comparison with its containing view. As a box's size gets close to the size of the containing window or screen, it becomes less effective at communicating the separation of grouped content.**" "**A box's border is a distinct visual element — adding nested boxes to define subgroups can make your interface feel busy and constrained**"; use "padding and alignment" instead. For tables it prefers banding: "the grouped style uses headers, footers, and additional space to separate groups of data… macOS defines a bordered style that uses alternating row backgrounds." Generally: "you might use **negative space, container shapes, or separator lines** to show which elements are related and which are unrelated."

**Polaris** rations depth ("Don't overuse depth, as it can lead to a cluttered and confusing interface") and adds an integrity rule: "**Don't allow elements to protrude outside of their parent containers. This disrupts the natural perception of depth and hierarchy.**" Its Card exists for this brief's problem — "Cards are used to group similar concepts and tasks together for merchants to scan, read, and get things done" — with "Use headings that set clear expectations about the card's purpose" and "**Stick to single user flows or break more complicated flows into multiple sections.**" Polaris nests *sections* — section, subdued section, titled section, subsection, flushed section — not whole cards.

**GOV.UK** has no card. Backlog issue #113, opened 15 Jan 2018, is still open, and its panel recommendation is the strongest published scepticism about containment: "**There were concerns that cards are often used to compensate for poor information hierarchy or content design.** Contributors… should establish what user needs the pattern is designed to meet and write appropriate guidance explaining when to use, and not to use, the pattern." Not a rejection — "When used appropriately, cards are a good way of chunking up related content, data and links" — but conditioned on consistency. The one card-like component shipped is the summary card, whose escalation rule puts headings first: "you can add structure by using headings or summary cards," with the exclusion "Do not use it for tabular data."

## Part C. What the research measures

**Common region is a distinct principle, and it beats proximity and similarity.** Palmer (*Cognitive Psychology*, 1992): "A new principle of grouping is proposed that is based on elements being located within a common region of space… this factor strongly influences perceived grouping and **is capable of overcoming the effects of other powerful grouping factors such as proximity and similarity**… Further demonstrations suggest that it is **dominated by the smallest background area and that it can follow a hierarchical embedding scheme**. It is argued that common region cannot be reduced to the effects of proximity, closure, or any other previously known factor." Two clauses carry a nesting policy: "dominated by the smallest background area" says the *innermost* enclosure wins — which is why a card inside a card reads as two groups, not one emphasised group — and "hierarchical embedding" says nesting is legible in principle, without saying how deep. Rubio et al. (*Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 2018) retested this without asking anyone to attend to grouping, using "an indirect task, i.e. repetition discrimination task": "**grouping by common region dominated the perceived organization of the display, even though the phenomenological strength of the grouping cues was equated for each participant by means of a preliminary scaling task.**"

**Grouping speeds search in a real interface.** Niemelä & Saarinen (*Human Factors*, 2000): "**Our results showed that both the presence of icons and their grouping had a significant positive effect on the scanning speed.**" Grouping is also one of four measurable properties of a dense display — Tullis (1983): "Four characteristics of display formats are described: **overall density, local density, grouping, and layout complexity**… these measures could provide the basis for objectively evaluating a display without collecting performance data." His 1981 comparison of narrative, structured/tabular and two graphic formats found "Accuracy did not significantly vary with format but speed did… **With additional practice, however, response times for the structured format were just as short.**" That practice effect matters for an all-day tool: structure that looks slower on first exposure catches up with training.

**Row banding is the best-measured containment device, and the effect is small.** Enders (*A List Apart*, 2008) ran three studies. The first found "no statistically significant difference in accuracy between striped answers and plain answers." The second added difficulty and time pressure ("Participants had 15 seconds to answer each question"), compared plain, lined and striped, and yielded **2,276 clean sessions**: "**zebra striping improved accuracy on three of the eight questions asked**… For the remaining four questions, the difference… cannot be statistically separated from random noise… **zebra striping doesn't harm performance—and in many cases, it actually leads to an improvement.**" Those results held "at not just p=0.05 but also p=0.01." The third, a Newspoll omnibus of "just over 1,200 Australians aged 18-64," found "**single-color, single-row… 31% of participants rated it as the table that helps the most and only 4% rated it as the table that helps the least**" (margin of error 2.8%). The most suggestive finding is behavioural: participants "spontaneously reported using their finger, on or over the computer screen, to follow down columns and across rows… **creating their own 'temporary' zebra striping**."

**Proximity has a measured effect size, and it scales with complexity.** Ginns's meta-analysis, via Klepsch et al. (*Frontiers in Education*, 2019, N₁=98, N₂=85, the original having no fetchable abstract): "Ginns (2006) supported the split-attention effect in a meta-analysis (**weighted mean effect size: d = 0.85; 95% CI 0.68–1.02**)… In the case of high complexity, spatial integration… results in high effect sizes (**d = 0.78**). When complexity is low, only small effect sizes (**d = 0.28; p > 0.05**) could be observed." Grouping pays roughly four times as much when material is complex — the dense-B2B case — and nothing measurable when it is simple. The same paper found closer is not monotonically better: "**unrelated representations should not be presented too close to each other, because the probability of micro-switches between unrelated representations increases with a high proximity (spatial contiguity failure)… Therefore, a medium proximity should be ideal**" — supported for transfer in Exp. 1 (p = 0.03) and for retention and transfer in Exp. 2 (p = 0.04; p = 0.01). A wall of rows *is* the high-proximity condition: everything is equally close to everything, so micro-switches between unrelated rows are cheap. On group size, Cowan (*BBS*, 2001) reports Miller's seven "was meant more as a rough estimate and a rhetorical device than as a real capacity limit… **A single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks is implicated.**"

**Containment can be over-applied, with measured costs.** NN/g on the illusion of completeness: "A break in content marked by a horizontal line that spans the width of the page can create a visual barrier and discourage people to scroll further… they can assume it's not just the end of a section, but also the end of the page," and from a usability study, "**Six out of eight users did not realize they could scroll down this page.**" On clutter: "using whitespace alone to create clear groupings reduces the visual complexity of a design. Borders are often added in an abundance of caution… however, this approach can result in busy, cluttered designs and in many situations it's enough to rely on proximity for grouping." Its diagnostic works as a test: "are they necessary to understand the grouping? Can I communicate this grouping by simply adding or removing whitespace?… Were users confused during usability testing when the boundaries were not present?"

**Cards versus lists.** Laubheimer (NN/g, 2016) defines a card as using "a border around the grouped content, and the background color within the card that differs from background color of the underlying canvas," then gives three arguments against cards for dense homogeneous data: "**Card layouts typically deemphasize the ranking of content**"; "**Card layouts are less scannable than lists.** A standard vertical list view… is more scannable than cards because the positioning of the individual elements is fixed in size and more predictable for the eye. Thus, cards are not appropriate when users search for a specific item from a list"; and "**Cards take more space.**" Scope: "Cards work best for collections of heterogeneous items… When presenting very homogenous items… consider using a standard vertical list."

**Baymard on cards in a dashboard.** The controlling variable is uniformity, not the container: "**the same types of content should have the highest visual consistency from card to card. Otherwise, users are likely to miss account features they're looking for, even if they're visible in the viewport, due to the overly 'busy' and 'cluttered' look of the dashboard.**" In one recorded failure a subject "paused with the link in the viewport for roughly three seconds, then scrolled back to the top of the page." Baymard's separate encapsulation finding carries the rarest caveat here: borders and background do raise perceived robustness, but "**if you use a similar style elsewhere… for less sensitive information, it will likely be considered generic styling and not yield the desired uptick.**" Containment is a contrast device; applied everywhere it says nothing.

## Part D. Nesting

No system forbids nesting; each caps it. **Carbon's cap is a number**: four layers, `<Layer>` enforcing them, nesting "up to three level, the last level corresponding to the 03 layer set tokens." Because a field counts as its own layer, an ordinary settings page with tabs, tiles and an input already reaches 03 — three is fewer than it sounds. **Atlassian's is a plane rule plus a quota**: sunken only on default, "at most one raised region per screen," overlays may stack on overlays, and its do/don't table pairs "Single card surface + `border`" against "**Nested card-on-card or stacked floating shadows everywhere**." **Apple's is a size heuristic plus a ban on visual subgroups**: a container nearly the size of the page stops working as one, and "adding nested boxes to define subgroups can make your interface feel busy and constrained." **Material's is a substitution rule**: "**Don't force content into cards when spacing, headlines, or dividers would create a simpler visual hierarchy.**" Inside a card the sanctioned subdivision is dividers — "Use full-width dividers for content that can be expanded… Use inset dividers, which don't run the full width of a card, to separate related content." M2 makes card-in-card incoherent by definition; M3 adds "Any overlapping containment areas or components should have different color roles," which is what the five surface container levels are for.

**Tables inside containers.** Only Carbon speaks directly, and says no: "**Avoid placing data tables inside data tables or smaller containers where the information can feel cramped or needs truncation**," and "consider giving your data table the most width on the page." Where a table must share a crowded grid its remedy is tone, not a box: "Use a hybrid grid or a dissimilar background color to avoid the components blending into each other." GOV.UK draws the same line from the other side ("Do not use it for tabular data"). Atlassian's only table containment rule is the scroll edge: prefer "the border-on-scroll treatment (`border` at the clip edge)… reach for `shadow-overflow` only when borders would be missed (horizontal table scroll)." Apple's equivalent is row banding, not enclosure.

**Forms inside containers.** Carbon alone is mechanical: a field is a layer above whatever it sits on, so a form in a tile is layer 02 by construction. Baymard gives the one measured reason to enclose *part* of a form, and the reason not to enclose all of it.

**Lists of cards versus one list container.** M3 allows cards "in a grid, vertical list, or carousel," but "On smaller screens… consider swapping cards for lists, which can display images and text in a more compact form." NN/g supplies the rule and the evidence: heterogeneous items justify cards, homogeneous rows do not. Carbon's group rule is uniformity — "Do not mix different variants of tiles in groups" — and Baymard's dashboard failure is that rule measured.

## Part E. Synthesis

### Agreed

1. Containment is a perceptual mechanism, not a style; enclosure beats proximity, similarity and connectedness.
2. Cheapest sufficient device, in order: **whitespace → divider or border → background tone → shadow**. Material, Atlassian, Fluent and NN/g say this in near-identical words.
3. Elevation and containment are different jobs — M3 explicitly, Carbon's unelevated tiles, Atlassian's sunken-groups/raised-moves split.
4. A flat container (border or tone, no shadow) is the desktop default: M2's 0dp stroked card, M3's filled and outlined cards at level 0, Atlassian's hairline border, Carbon's ban on tile shadows.
5. Shadow is for two jobs only: separation from a busy background, and interaction feedback.
6. Overlapping containers must differ in colour role.
7. Nesting tops out around three levels, and the innermost enclosure wins.
8. A container nearly the size of the page is not a container.
9. Containment must be rationed to keep its meaning (Baymard, Polaris, NN/g).
10. Uniformity within a set of containers matters more than the containers (Baymard, Carbon).
11. Containers suit heterogeneous items; long homogeneous rows want a list with headings and dividers.
12. Full-width horizontal boundaries can stop scrolling (six of eight users).
13. Group size is about four, not seven (Cowan) — items per container and containers per region alike.
14. The payoff scales with complexity (d = 0.78 high, 0.28 n.s. low), so a dense B2B app is where containment earns most.

### Disagreements

- **What carries depth.** Fluent: shadow only. Carbon: value steps only. M3: tone by default. Apple: translucency. Atlassian: surface *and* shadow, mandatorily paired.
- **Whether elevation may group.** Atlassian: sunken groups, raised does not. M3: surface roles group, elevation does not. M1: shadow is the only cue at all.
- **What the page background is.** Atlassian: white canvas, bordered cards — "grey page, white cards" named as drift. Carbon: base then layer 01, i.e. White on Gray 10 in a light theme — exactly that. Direct opposites.
- **Whether a card needs a shadow.** NN/g's definition includes one; Carbon and Atlassian forbid it by default; M3 makes it one of three variants — "Each provides the same legibility and functionality, so the variant you use depends on style alone."
- **Whether cards belong in product UI.** Polaris builds its admin on them; GOV.UK has not shipped one in eight years; Atlassian names "floating cards everywhere" an anti-pattern.
- **Whether tonal elevation works.** M3 shipped it in 2021 and has now deprecated it; Atlassian and Carbon still derive dark themes from exactly that mechanism.
- **Closer versus contained.** Klepsch et al. found medium proximity beat high; the split-attention literature pushes for maximum integration. No system acknowledges the trade-off.
- **What a boundary costs.** NN/g treats full-width boundaries as a scroll hazard; Carbon, Material and Polaris use full-width section dividers freely.

### Still unknown

- **No controlled study compares border versus whitespace versus background tint in a dense product UI.** Palmer's stimuli were circles; Niemelä & Saarinen varied spatial grouping, not enclosure style; the zebra studies varied banding and rules, never enclosure. Every system's ordering rule is asserted, not measured.
- **No measured ceiling on nesting depth**; Carbon's three is an implementation limit.
- **No measured effect of a container around a table.** The nearest evidence is banding *inside* one: three of eight questions across 2,276 sessions — real but small.
- **Whether Tullis's practice effect applies to containment** — whether trained daily users still benefit after weeks, or whether containment is mainly a first-week aid.
- **Whether a container boundary has a contrast floor.** M2 requires "accessible contrast ratios"; WCAG 1.4.11's 3:1 covers "user interface components and states"; nobody says whether a decorative section boundary is in scope.
- **Whether a table inside a card is actually worse.** Carbon asserts it; nobody measured it.
- **Whether Atlassian or Carbon is right about the page canvas.** Opposite defaults, equal confidence, no evidence.
- **Why Material deprecated surface tint.** The spec records the decision and publishes no rationale.
