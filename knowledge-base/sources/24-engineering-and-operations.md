# 24. Engineering and operations

Two domains: issue tracking and project work (Jira, Linear, Asana, GitHub Issues), and incident response and observability (PagerDuty, Datadog, Grafana, Opsgenie, incident.io). For each: objects and relations, the chains people run most as H2 move sequences with an H3 shape and a frequency estimate, where the software documentedly cuts them, and what the model cannot express. Then the H1-H5 verdicts.

Source tiers, labelled inline: **tier 1** peer-reviewed; **tier 2** industry research or preprints with a published methodology; **tier 3** dated practitioner and vendor material. Vendor documentation is weak evidence for what work *is* and strong evidence for what was *broken*: a documented limit is a chain cut written down by its builders.

## The measured background for both domains

No click-count literature exists here, but the switching telemetry is good and sets the frequency scale for every chain below. Instrumenting 20 developers' computers at four companies for ~11 work days each, Meyer et al. found "developers' work is highly fragmented, as developers are spending only very short amounts of time (0.3 to 2 minutes) in one activity before switching to another one" (*IEEE TSE* 43(12), 2017; tier 1). With eye tracking over ~17 days each, 18 developers "focused on a specific window for only 15.7 (±77.3, median=2.2) seconds, before switching to the next one", kept "12.1 (±6.6) windows open at all times from running an average of 9.5 (±3.0) applications", and reported "1.8 (±0.9) windows to be relevant out of the 14.5 (±11.8) windows that were open" (Pilzer et al., 2020; tier 2, venue unverified). From 5,971 Microsoft responses, "developers had on average 4.66 interruptions on their past workday" and "the longest period developers could work on code without an interruption was 47.3 minutes, on average", with resumption self-reported at 8.64 minutes (Meyer et al., 2019; tier 1). Atlassian and DX, surveying 3,500 developers and managers, add the week-scale residue: "Developers only spend 16% of their time coding, and coding is not a friction point for developers", "50% report losing 10+ hours per week", and the top time-wasters are "finding information (services, docs, APIs), adapting new technology, and context switching between tools" (2025; tier 2).

# Part A. Issue tracking and project work

## A1. Object types and relations

The object set is small and near-identical across products: **issue**, **parent/epic**, **sub-issue**, **project**, **cycle** or **sprint**, **team**, **person**, **status**, **label**, **priority**, **estimate**, **comment** — and, reaching into the other tool, **branch**, **commit**, **pull request**, **build**, **deployment**. Relations: issue→assignee; issue→status *through a workflow*; issue→parent and children; issue→project and cycle; issue→blocking/blocked-by; issue→duplicate-of; issue→customer request; issue→branch/commit/PR/build/deploy. Above these sit two aggregates people treat as objects: the **queue** (triage inbox, backlog, "my issues", a saved filter) and the **board**.

Three structural facts matter more than the list. The busiest relation, issue→code, is stored by neither side: it is *asserted by a string convention* in a branch name, commit message or PR body. Mutating a status is gated by a workflow authored elsewhere. And the surfaces people live on are query results, so acting from them risks invalidating the thing they are standing on.

## A2. The chains

An upper bound on chain count: in a five-day diary study of 18 Google engineers, "diaries contained an average of 18 entries per day with an average of 25 minutes elapsed between entries" (Brown et al., *CHI '23*; tier 1). Cadences below come from the artefact, from published rates — at Google "the median developer authors about 3 changes a week... the median for changes reviewed by developers per week is 4" (Sadowski et al., ICSE-SEIP '18, from "log data... pertaining to 9 million reviews over two years"; tier 1) — or from a vendor spending a keystroke on a move.

**A1-A2. Triage an incoming issue, and mark duplicates** — read, decide it is real, label, route, prioritise, or merge into an existing issue. *Lap over a queue with an occasional fork*, tens per day for whoever holds the rotation. Linear built the feature after watching customers do the chain by hand: some "had started building separate Triage 'teams' where they would file all new issues, then clean up, prioritize, and move them to the appropriate team" (linear.app/changelog, 29 Jun 2021). Its shortcuts now name the moves — "_accept_ with `1`, _mark as duplicate_ with `2`, _decline_ with `3`, or _snooze_ with `H`" — and marking duplicate "will also move the new issue's attachments to the canonical issue" (linear.app/docs/triage, fetched 18 Sep 2026).

**A3-A4. Park it until something arrives, or route it by rule.** The snooze is the only first-class *chain over time* I found in either domain: it returns the issue "at a time of your choosing, or when there's new activity on that issue: whichever comes first". The *configure* move recurses: "when moving issues to another team's Triage via rule, the new team's rules are applied to the issue as well" (same page).

**A5-A7.** **Groom the backlog** — read, estimate, re-rank, split, defer, close (*lap over dozens of objects*, weekly). A practitioner's description of the cut is exact: "I open an epic... I hit the 'Hide done'. Then I thoughtlessly click on one of the stories, I edit it, go back to the epic, and now it shows all the stories again" (Hacker News, 31 May 2023; tier 3). **Plan the cycle** — collect, compare capacity with estimates, verify the total (*fork*, fortnightly). **Stand-up update** — read my issues, verify state, report (*lap then communicate*, daily).

**A8-A9. Start work and link the pull request** — assign to self, mutate status, create a branch encoding the issue, then link the PR back. *Hop across products plus a link*, a few times a day per engineer, both halves riding a string convention; Linear reduces the first to a clipboard action, copying the branch name with "`Cmd/Ctrl` + `Shift` + `.`" to paste into git (linear.app/docs/github, fetched 18 Sep 2026). GitHub documents how the second breaks: keywords "are interpreted only when the pull request targets the repository's default branch... these keywords are ignored, no links are created, and merging the PR has no effect on the issues"; from the PR side "the issue and pull request must be in the same repository"; and undoing a keyword link means editing the PR description (docs.github.com, fetched 18 Sep 2026).

**A10-A12.** **Let the merge move the status** (*ripple across products*) — a closing magic word moves the issue "to the status configured for _On PR or commit merge_", a relation word "simply marks the PR as related to the issue, with no status changes" (linear.app/docs/github); Jira's version is irreversible — "once development information has been linked to a work items, it can't be removed" — and its commit-driven transitions fail quietly: "If any other fields have been set as required the transition command will silently fail" (support.atlassian.com, smart commits, fetched 18 Sep 2026). **Create an issue from a pull request** — "Add `{TEAM}-NEW` to a pull request description to create and link an issue in that team." **Check the code from the issue** (*traverse a relation*, many times a day for leads) — Jira's development panel gives "just enough information to evaluate the status of a work item's development at a glance", then two clicks leave for the connected tool; it needs its own permission, and "only the first 100 commits linked to a work item will be shown" (support.atlassian.com, fetched 18 Sep 2026).

**A13. Wait for review, then resume** — *chain over time*, 3-4 times a week per engineer, and the largest manufacturer of switches in the domain. At Google "the overall (all code sizes) median latency for the entire review process is under 4 hours", and Google is the fast case, "significantly lower than the median time to approval reported by Rigby and Bird... which is 17.5 hours for AMD" and 14.7 to 19.8 hours across three Microsoft projects (Sadowski et al., 2018). Most is dead time: "Nudge was able to reduce pull request resolution time by 60% for 8,500 pull requests" in a randomized trial on 147 Microsoft repositories (Maddila et al., arXiv:2011.12468; tier 2) — blocked not on effort but on nobody being told the chain was open.

**A14. Sweep a set of issues** — collect a set, then mutate it in one act. Jira: "You can only edit 1000 work items at once"; with mixed workflows "you can only transition them in groups with the same workflow, and one group at a time"; and from the backlog, assignee, priority, estimate and labels each carry a "limit of 50 work items at a time" (support.atlassian.com, bulk edit, fetched 18 Sep 2026).

**A15-A19.** **What is blocking me** — traverse blocked-by into another team's issue, verify its status (*fork across teams*, daily for leads). In Jira the traversal can be invisible: when a developer with access to only one project opens their story, "the dependency doesn't show up in the dependencies sections. He would have to be a SL project developper too to see the dependency", and the sanctioned fix is a per-user permission grant the thread itself calls "very manual" (Atlassian Community, 25 Aug 2023; tier 3). **Report upward or answer "what shipped"** — collect across projects and cycles, compare with the plan and the deploy list, communicate outward (*fork across products*, weekly). **Re-plan after a scope change** — mutate one estimate, see what moved (*ripple*, weekly). **Hand over across time zones** — comment with enough state for the next person (*handoff*, daily).

**A20-A21. Resume my own work, and keep two trackers in step.** Resumption, measured on "10,000 recorded sessions of 86 programmers and a survey of 414 programmers": "only 10% of the sessions have programming activity resume in less than 1 min after an interruption, only 7% of the programming sessions involve no navigation to other locations prior to editing" (Parnin and Rugaber, 2011; tier 1) — at 4.66 interruptions a day, several times daily, nearly always needing re-traversal first. Sync is *configure a rule, then live with the leaks*: "any custom statuses set at the GitHub Project level do not sync to Linear", with errors surfaced as a banner (linear.app/docs/github).

## A3. Where the software cuts these chains

1. **Issue→code is a convention, not an edge** — typed into a branch, commit or PR body; silently void on a non-default branch; irreversible once made (Jira); undone only by editing a different object (GitHub).
2. **The sweep is broken back into laps by the workflow model** (Jira's per-workflow, one-transition, 1000-item limits), and **the traversal into code is permission-gated, truncated and ends in another product**.
3. **Acting from a queue costs the queue** (A5). Linear rebuilt its inbox "so that you can review the notification and update the issue without losing context or having to move back and forth from the Inbox view" (changelog, 2 Dec 2021); Jira shipped a hierarchy flyout that works "without losing your place in the timeline" (Atlassian Cloud changelog, Sep 2026). Both are the cut, admitted.
4. **Per-action clicks, counted by the people paying them.** When Jira grouped attach/link/create behind an "+Add" button, its own PM wrote that the menu's growth "has led to cognitive overload and negatively affected task completion" (Atlassian Community, 15 Oct 2024); a user replied, "I link issues and create sub-tasks all day long, so those extra clicks really add up" (31 Oct 2024).
5. **The residue does not respond to better code tools.** DORA's 2025 report (≈5,000 professionals, fielded 13 Jun - 21 Jul 2025; tier 2) found AI adoption has "No relationship with friction": "friction doesn't vanish so much as move: It shifts from manual grind to deciding and verifying", and "a poor developer experience and fragmented tooling may hamper the impacts of your AI strategy."

Gap stated honestly: Asana's help centre is a JavaScript-only application that returned no article text to any fetch, so Part A rests on Linear, GitHub, Atlassian's help centre and dated community threads.

# Part B. Incident response and observability

## B1. Object types and relations

**Service** is the hub: owners (**team**, **person**), an **on-call schedule** and **escalation path**, **deploys**, dependencies, and telemetry — **metrics**, **logs**, **traces**, **events**. A **monitor** emits **alerts**; alerts deduplicate into groups, escalate to whoever is on call, and are promoted into an **incident** with severity, status, **responders** in roles, a **timeline**, **status updates** to subscribers or a status page, linked **signals** (the graph or query that showed it), and afterwards a **postmortem** with **follow-ups** that become issues in Part A's tracker.

Nearly every relation is many-to-many and crosses product boundaries: alert→service, service→escalation path→schedule→person, incident→signals, incident→suspect deploys, incident→affected customers, incident→chat channel, postmortem→follow-up→issue→pull request. Two are never stored and are re-derived by hand every time: *alert→the change that caused it*, and *this incident→the one that looked like this last month*. 

Vendors describe the graph in the same words. Grafana: "Keep on-call and incident response alongside your observability data... you can investigate with the same metrics, logs, and traces that fired the alert" (grafana.com/docs, IRM). Datadog: "Incidents live in Datadog alongside your metrics, traces, and logs" (docs.datadoghq.com). Each sentence sells the absence of a hop.

## B2. The chains

Frequency rests on a published target: an on-call incident's tasks take "6 hours. It follows that the maximum number of incidents per day is 2 per 12-hour on-call shift... with a likely median value of 0" (*Site Reliability Engineering*, ch. 11, Google, 2017; tier 2). Whole-incident chains therefore run 0-2 per shift when healthy; diagnostic sub-chains inside one incident run tens of times an hour. Reality exceeds the target: in Grafana Labs' Observability Survey (1,363 responses, 1 Oct 2025 - 10 Jan 2026, Censuswide; tier 2), "30% of respondents cite alert fatigue" as the biggest obstacle to faster incident response, "the most common response by a wide margin".

**B1. Page to acknowledgement** — read alert, verify it is real, mutate status, say you have it. *Hop*, 0-2 per shift. The page carries a threshold, not a situation: "many paging notifications provide limited insights, requiring you to jump over to your troubleshooting tools" (Datadog blog, 10 Jun 2025; tier 3).

**B2. Alert to first hypothesis** — traverse to the service, read dashboards, compare with last week, collect anomalies, decide where to look. *Fork*, tens of times per incident. Process tracing of real incidents found the earliest activity was not hypothesis-driven at all but "sweeping looks across the environment looking for cues" (*STELLA report*, SNAFUcatchers, Mar 2017; tier 2) — which one-object-per-screen cannot serve.

**B3. Metric to trace to log** — read a spike, traverse to an exemplar trace, then to that span's logs, compare with a healthy request. *Hop, repeated*, dozens of times per incident. The one traversal vendors have engineered, and the engineering is a precondition: logs "*must* be turned into Datadog attributes in order for traces and logs correlation to work", and it fails silently when sampling diverges (docs.datadoghq.com, fetched 18 Sep 2026). Grafana makes the relation an administered object — "a correlation defines how data in one data source is used to query data in another data source" — with external targets opening "in a new tab" (grafana.com/docs, fetched 18 Sep 2026).

**B4-B5. What changed, and declare the incident** — traverse to deploys, compare deploy times against the break in the graph, read the diff, decide to roll back; then create the incident from whatever showed it. *Fork then decide, then create from context*, once or twice per incident, and the highest-yield chain in the domain. Google's doctrine starts there: systems "have inertia", so examine "recent changes to a system", correlating latency against "deployment start and end times" (*SRE*, ch. 12, 2017) — yet the edge alert→deploy is stored by no product and is re-derived by eye from two timelines in two tools. Datadog has at least removed the re-keying at declaration: declare "from a graph widget on a dashboard, the Incidents UI, or any alert reporting into Datadog", and "the graph is added to the incident as a signal" (docs.datadoghq.com, fetched 18 Sep 2026).

**B6-B7.** **Assemble the responders** — traverse service→owner→escalation path→schedule→person, communicate, verify they answered: *handoff*. incident.io documents the traversal as the design — "your alert should be able to resolve to an escalation path via Catalog" (docs.incident.io, fetched 18 Sep 2026) — and Grafana keeps the handoff state as data: a participant's "response status (invited, acknowledged, accepted, or declined)". **Run the runbook** — *detour*: the chain stops, a procedure runs, the chain must resume at the same point; PagerDuty's configured form applies "'if-this-then-that' logic" through triggers of four types (support.pagerduty.com, fetched 18 Sep 2026) — a rule authored far from the incident it acts on.

**B8-B9. Keep stakeholders informed, or delegate the looking** — read state, compose, communicate outward, return to diagnosis; or hand the sub-chain to someone else. *Detour, or handoff of a sub-chain*, several times per incident, and the domain's most expensive move. Google prices it in the responder's own currency: the VPs "are nagging you for an ETA... You would sympathize, but doing so would require cognitive effort that you are holding in reserve for your job" (*SRE*, ch. 14, 2017). STELLA makes it a dilemma: specialists "constantly being dunned for explanations... are likely to make little progress on understanding the anomaly". Both vendors now sell the detour's removal: "Take action on your incident, all without leaving Slack" (PagerDuty); "update these pages in the same place that you conduct incident management, minimizing context switching" (Datadog).

**B10. Mitigate and verify** — mutate, verify, compare with baseline, decide whether to continue: *ripple*, the effect landing on another screen on a lag, since "the interventions that responders make are experiments that test their mental models" (STELLA, 2017).

**B11. Shift handoff mid-incident** — *handoff over time*, with a spoken protocol because the tools do not carry the state: the outgoing commander "should be explicit in their handoff, specifically stating, 'You're now the incident commander, okay?'". The artefact holding the working set sits outside the stack by design — "keep a living incident document... Most of our teams use Google Docs" — since "depending on the software you are trying to fix... is unlikely to end well" (*SRE*, ch. 14, 2017).

**B12-B14. Tune the alert, deduplicate the storm, reconstruct the timeline.** Tuning is a *hop into configuration*, daily, with the rule authored far from the 3 a.m. evidence that justified it; dedup is a *lap* whose residue is the 30% alert-fatigue figure; and the write-up is a *chain over time*, once per incident, collecting across chat, alerts, deploys, graphs and shell history hours or days after the acts. Google's postmortem group's job includes "automating postmortem creation with data from tools used during an incident" (*SRE*, ch. 15, 2017), and chat is load-bearing because it is the only surface that recorded the chain, while face-to-face exchanges "are not routinely recorded" (STELLA, 2017).

**B15. Follow-ups into the backlog** — create from context, link, hand off to the tracker, verify weeks later. *Handoff across products, then chain over time.* incident.io documents the cut and its repair: capture one "without breaking off what you're doing"; paste an existing ticket's link and it "is connected to that issue from the start, so its status stays in sync without anyone copying anything across" (docs.incident.io, fetched 18 Sep 2026). The price of the link is a mode: "once a follow-up is exported, the tracker owns its title, description, status, owner, and labels."

**B16-B19.** **Is this the same as last time?** — *fork across time*, weekly; nothing indexes incidents by symptom shape, so people search chat. **Who is affected?** — service→customers, then the support queue (*fork across three products*). **Postmortem to prevention** — read, decide, configure alerts and SLOs, verify weeks later (*chain over time with a long lag*). **The command-line detour** — under pressure experts leave the object screens entirely, because "the automation and monitoring applications are treated, by comparison, as being indirect or even opaque" (STELLA, 2017).

## B3. Where the software cuts these chains

1. **The page arrives without its evidence** (B1-B2) — notifications "requiring you to jump over to your troubleshooting tools" (Datadog, Jun 2025) — and **traversal between telemetry types is a configured, fallible, tab-opening edge** (B3).
2. **Communicating outward evicts the diagnostician** (B8) — "cognitive effort that you are holding in reserve for your job"; both vendors' fixes pitched as "without leaving Slack" and "minimizing context switching".
3. **State the chain needed is destroyed and re-collected later** (B14) — timelines rebuilt from chat; Google automating postmortem creation "with data from tools used during an incident".
4. **The working set never coexists on one surface.** Graph, trace, deploy, runbook step, customer impact and paged person live in different products; what the tools keep instead is a *timeline* — the working set flattened into history, no longer usable as a working set.

# Part C. The model, hypothesis by hypothesis

**H1. The work is a graph, the software is a tree — holds, with one amendment.** Both domains are small object sets with dense many-to-many relations, shipped as one screen per object type: issue view, backlog, board, roadmap; alert list, dashboard, trace explorer, schedule, incident page. Every chain crosses at least one boundary and the valuable ones cross three or four — 9.5 concurrent applications and 12.1 windows, 1.8 of them relevant (Pilzer et al., 2020). The amendment: the software is not one tree but *several trees owned by different vendors*, and the most valuable edges span them — alert→deploy, incident→follow-up→issue→pull request, issue→branch→review. Those edges are absent or exist only as an integration administered in advance (Grafana's correlations, Datadog's tag injection, Linear's magic words, incident.io's auto-export), so the mismatch is partly organisational and no single product can repair it.

**H2. The moves — holds; the list is close to complete.** Every chain decomposes into the eleven moves without strain, and the two least glamorous are the most frequent: *verify a state* (is the alert real, did the rollback work, did the linked issue close) and *configure a rule that governs many objects* (triage rules, workflows, escalation paths, correlations). Two refinements the evidence forces: *communicate outward* should split into telling a known person now and writing a record for an unknown future reader; and *collect a set* and *mutate a set in one act* are separate moves, Jira's bulk-change limits being the cost of the second.

**H3. The chain shapes — hold, with two additions.** All seven recur with the predicted failure modes; the detour and handoff are the most vividly documented (Google's spoken handoff protocol, STELLA's delegated data-gathering, the status-update detour). Additions: the **sweep**, one act intended to apply to a whole set — not a fast lap but a different intent (A14); and the **pre-hypothesis scan**, "sweeping looks across the environment looking for cues" (STELLA, 2017), a shape with no target object at all.

**H4. Indirection is the cost — holds, with good field evidence, but needs a second dimension.** That experts under pressure abandon sophisticated monitoring for the terminal because those applications "are treated, by comparison, as being indirect or even opaque" is a direct observation of practitioners choosing the lower-indirection instrument as stakes rise, and vendor copy prices indirection identically ("jump over to your troubleshooting tools", "without leaving Slack"). What indirection alone does not predict: **authority distance** — after export "the tracker owns its title, description, status, owner, and labels", so the nearest surface is no longer the one allowed to act; **waiting**, where the cost is elapsed time with nobody acting and a notification recovered 60% of it; and **pressure as a multiplier**. Cost is a function of the situation, not only of geometry.

**H5. The properties of perfect — holds, and gains one measured endorsement.** Five of seven have vendor documentation behind them: effects where the cause was (declare from a graph and the graph becomes a signal); reason and act on one surface ("all without leaving Slack"); the working set staying visible (on-call "alongside your observability data"); no move destroying needed state (Linear's conditional snooze, its inbox rebuilt so you act "without losing context"); and the software knowing which chain you are in (workflows keyed to incident type). The measured endorsement: from logs and surveys of 13,383 engineers, "the total hours spent in focus time in a quarter was not a significant predictor of self-reported focus and flow... The total number of focus sessions in a quarter was a positive predictor" (Brown et al., *CHI '23*) — intact chains, not total time, predict felt flow. The two properties with no supporting evidence are *cost proportional to cognitive weight* and *a detour returns to the exact point*: nothing in either domain describes returning a person to the position they left, and the timeline artefact preserves history instead of position. Parnin and Rugaber's 10% and 7% are the consequence.

# Part D. Outside the model

1. **The pre-hypothesis scan.** Read, traverse and compare presuppose a target; the first minutes of an incident and the first pass over a queue are a search for something worth pointing at.
2. **Absorbing someone else's interruption.** STELLA's bystanders, who gather data "as a means of reducing context switching" for the responders, are neither taking the chain over nor helping with it; they are *shielding* it — work whose product is another person's uncut chain.
3. **Instrument credibility.** Practitioners re-verify in the shell what the dashboard already said; distance does not explain that, trust does.
4. **Re-derived relations.** Alert→causing change and this-incident→that-incident are stored nowhere and recomputed by eye; issue→code is the same with a string as carrier. The model assumes a relation exists to traverse and has no cost term for *inferring* one.
5. **Authority moving with the link.** Linking an object into another system can transfer the right to edit it — a mode created by a relation, not a UI state.
6. **Writing for an unknown future reader.** Timelines, postmortems and stand-up notes are produced during the chain for people not in it, and paid for by whoever is deepest in the work.
7. **Holding a chain open while waiting.** Review queues and acknowledgement timeouts suspend a chain for hours or days; the model covers resumption, not the cost of keeping the intention alive.
8. **Several people in one chain at once, mostly re-reading.** All seven shapes are single-person, and 76.6% of window switches go to an already-open window (Pilzer et al., 2020).
9. **Silent failure of a move.** A GitHub keyword on a non-default branch, a Jira smart commit blocked by a required field ("the transition command will silently fail"), or a trace ID whose trace was sampled away produces no error and no effect — the model prices moves, not moves that appear to succeed.

# Part E. Still unknown

- **No click-count study exists for either domain.** The switching telemetry is excellent on rhythm and silent on chains: nobody has instrumented "triage one issue" or "diagnose one alert" end to end and counted the moves, and the click counts quoted above are dated practitioner complaints, not measurements. Chain frequency is likewise anchored only where a target or rate was published: Google's "2 incidents per 12-hour shift" is a goal, the review rates are one company, and per-day counts of triage and grooming laps are inferred from cadence.
- **The cost of one telemetry traversal is unmeasured** — no published time-to-pivot from metric to trace to log, with and without correlation configured — and so is postmortem reconstruction. Asana's help centre and Opsgenie's end-of-life terms were not reachable.
- **Perceived and measured costs diverge.** DORA 2025 cites METR finding "developers who were slowed down by AI tools by 19% still believed the tools had made them 20% more efficient" — a warning about every self-reported figure here.
- **Four sources could not be fetched, and their claims are not used.** Grayson's and Maguire's papers (*CACM*/*ACM Queue* 63(4), Apr 2020) and the SPACE paper all returned 403, so SPACE's statements on flow are absent here; Microsoft's Work Trend Index served no page body; the VOID report is PDF only. STELLA carries the anomaly-response weight instead, a substitute of slightly lower standing.
- **No product has published a before-and-after.** Every vendor claim of reduced switching is an assertion; only Nudge and Pilzer's window-dimming trial report controlled effects.
